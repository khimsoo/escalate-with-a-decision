# Escalate with a Decision

Source: framework adapted from a 6-card post by The Executive Comms Insider. Rewritten in my own words for reuse.

## Core idea

Do not hand your boss a problem. Hand them a decision.
A message that only describes what went wrong passes the thinking upward. Senior people need what happened, what it means, and what you recommend.

## When to use

- I paste or describe an email or message I'm about to send to a boss, sponsor, minister, or other senior stakeholder that reports a problem, risk, delay, or incident.
- I ask "how should I escalate this?" or "can you tighten this?"
- I paste a message from my own team that raises an issue and ask how to respond (use Mode B).

Skip for routine FYIs, good news, or messages with no issue or ask.

## The five questions (the readiness gate)

Before anything is escalated, the message must answer all five:

1. **What happened?** Facts only, in one or two lines.
2. **So what?** Impact: timeline, customer, cost, risk, reputation. Quantify where possible.
3. **What are the options?** Usually two or three, with trade-offs.
4. **What do I recommend?** One option, with the reason.
5. **What do I need from them?** A specific decision, approval or action, and a deadline.

If any of the five is missing, the sender may not be ready to escalate. Say which are missing and ask for them, or flag them as gaps in the draft.

## Mode A: Rewrite my outgoing escalation

1. Read the draft or situation. Map what's there against the five questions.
2. Name the gaps in one short line (e.g. "No options, no recommendation, no deadline").
3. Rewrite using the template below. Keep it short and use the sender's own facts. Never invent numbers, causes, dates or options; mark them `[X]` or ask me.
4. Add a one-line check: which of the five were filled, and which still need my input.

### Template (full)

> **Subject:** [Issue] – decision needed by [time/date]
>
> **What happened:** …
> **What it means:** … (impact, with numbers)
> **Options:** A) … B) … C) …
> **My recommendation:** Option _ because …
> **What I need from you:** [specific approval/decision] by [deadline].

### Template (early warning, when I don't have the full answer yet)

Don't wait for the perfect solution. If the risk is material, leadership needs visibility early.

> Here is what we know. Here is the immediate risk. Here is what we're doing now. I'll update you by [time].

Acknowledging what is not yet known is still ownership. The goal is a clear point of view, not all the answers.

## Mode B: Respond to an escalation from my team

When someone brings me a bare problem, don't absorb it. Reply with a coaching prompt:

- Ask them the five questions, especially "what do you recommend?" and "what do you need from me?"
- Offer the junior-to-senior rewrite so they can see the pattern.
- Where the risk is real and time-sensitive, take the early-warning route first and ask for the full version after.

## Junior vs senior escalation

| Junior | Senior |
|---|---|
| "Here's the problem." | "Here's the problem. Here's my read. Here's my recommendation. Here's the decision I need." |

Your boss should not have to do your thinking before they can do theirs.

## Weak vs strong (example)

- Weak: "We have a problem with the launch." It forces the boss to ask what happened, how serious it is, the cause, the options, and what you need.
- Strong: "The launch is at risk by two weeks because X happened. We have three options. I recommend Option B because it protects the customer commitment with the lowest commercial impact. I need your approval by 3pm."

## Self-check before sending

- [ ] Could the reader act on this without replying with a question?
- [ ] Is there exactly one recommendation?
- [ ] Is the ask specific, with a deadline?
- [ ] Is the subject line decision-oriented?
- [ ] Is it short enough to read on a phone?

## Tone

Calm, factual, owning the issue. No blame, no drama, no burying the ask. Lead with the headline; put detail below it.
